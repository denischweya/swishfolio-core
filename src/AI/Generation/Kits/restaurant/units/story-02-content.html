The place

It started with a borrowed grill.

For two summers before there was a restaurant there was a trailer, a grill welded from a ship’s hatch and a queue along the harbour wall. Inés cooked sardines and flatbreads; Tom poured sherry from a cool box and pretended it was a wine list.

When the tannery’s weighing room came up for lease (brick, iron columns, a chimney nobody had used since 1958) we signed before we had worked out how to pay for it. A blacksmith in Somerset built the hearth. Friends sanded the tables. The chimney, it turned out, still drew beautifully.

“We do not have a signature dish. We have a fire, and it has moods.”

Cooking this way is slower and less predictable than cooking on gas, and that is the point. The fire is lit at one o’clock and fed through the afternoon. By service there are three heats to cook on: flame, ember and ash. By midnight the bread for tomorrow is baking in what is left.

The room is small on purpose. Eleven tables means the kitchen can see every one of them, and it means that when we say the menu changes daily, it really does.
